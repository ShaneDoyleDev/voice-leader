# Voice Leader

**[Open it here](https://shanedoyledev.github.io/voice-leader/)** — it runs in the browser, with nothing to install.

An interactive SATB (soprano/alto/tenor/bass) voice-leading trainer. Pick a key and a chord progression, place each voice's notes on the staff, and get checked against the standard rules of four-part writing — the same way a theory instructor would mark it.

## What it does

- **Pick the key from a circle of fifths.** Majors on the outer ring with their accidental counts, relative minors inside; click a wedge to choose the key and the mode in one move, and the hub draws the signature as notation — a treble clef with its accidentals, not a list of letters. The six o'clock wedge carries the enharmonic pair — click it again to flip F♯/G♭ or D♯m/E♭m. Minor automatically raises the leading tone in V and vii°, no manual accidentals needed.
- **Build a progression.** Chain together any of the seven diatonic triads — I ii iii IV V vi vii° in major, i ii° III iv V VI vii° in minor — in whatever order you want, up to eight chords. Each numeral shows its own spelling in the current key before you add it.
- **Or take an exercise.** Two buttons under **Begin voicing** hand you a drill instead of a blank staff. **Figured bass** writes a bass line and asks for the tenor, alto and soprano above it; **Harmonise a melody** writes a soprano line and asks for the three voices under it. Each comes with its own key and progression — four to six chords, opening on the tonic and closing V–I — and the given line is written to be worth answering. It is given a shape before it is given notes — an arch, a climb, a descent into the cadence — as a target register for each chord in turn, so the line goes somewhere instead of circling the same three notes; then it is filled in with the chord tones nearest that shape, moving by step where it can and never leaping beyond a sixth. Each note looks a chord ahead as well: a letter appears only once inside a voice's range — the bass has one D to choose from, the soprano one B — so a line that ignored what was coming could strand itself with nothing but a seventh to leap, and this one will not stand where the next note is out of reach. A given bass stands on the root of every chord — the exercise is the three voices above it, not the reading of an inversion — and a leading tone in a given soprano resolves up a step to the tonic. The given voice is fixed: it draws in grey, takes no drag or double-click, and **Clear notes** leaves it where it is. Everything else is as usual — the figures still come off whatever is lowest, so a harmonised melody figures itself from the bass you write, and the checklist grades the answer exactly as it grades free voicing. **Reroll exercise** deals a new one; changing the key or the progression ends the drill and hands the staff back.
- **Nothing sounds as you write.** Placing a note, dragging one and clearing the staff are all silent. A chord is heard when you ask for it — **Play**, or its chip in the readout — on a sampled grand piano.
- **Wrong notes show in red.** A note that is not one of the chord's three tones draws in red on the staff, so a misplaced voice is obvious before you check anything.
- **The bass figures the numeral.** Once a chord is right — three or four notes, every one a tone of the chord, with its root and third both there — its lowest note figures it: the root leaves it plain, the third makes it I⁶, the fifth I⁶₄, stacked as figured bass, on the staff, in the readout and throughout the checklist. A chord still being built, or one with a wrong or missing note, goes unfigured until it is right.
- **The chord spelling keeps score.** Under each Roman numeral sits the chord's spelling; each tone lights up once you have placed it, in any octave, so you can see at a glance which tone the chord is still missing. The numeral itself lights up once the root and third are there, and a dot under a tone means you have doubled it.
- **The opening chord fixes the voices.** Once the first chord is right — four notes, every one of them a tone of the chord, its root and third both there — they are labelled S, A, T and B down the left, top note first, and those four voices hold for the rest of the progression. The letters go while a note is in hand and come back when it lands, so they never chase a note around. Only this chord carries the letters; every later chord takes its voices when it first fills up, by the assignment that moves each line least, and then keeps them. So a note dragged past another inside a later chord stays the voice it was — a real crossing, named as one — while the opening chord always re-reads top down, since it is the chord the others are fixed to. Lose a note and that chord unfixes until it is whole again. Every check below follows these voices rather than the pitch order, and the readout under the staff names each note's voice, so a crossing is plain there too.
- **Hear it back.** **Play** sits in the staff toolbar, there throughout, so a part-written progression can be heard as you go. It walks the chords one at a time — whatever voices are in each sounding together, the column lit and the chord's own notes warming to the accent colour over a quarter of a second and cooling back when it passes, an empty chord passing as a silent beat. A note that is not one of the chord's tones stays red through it: being wrong outranks being played. **Tempo** next to it sets how long each chord is held, from 40 to 168 to the beat — a chord a second at the default 60. Moving it mid-playback takes effect from the next chord, and during playback a note is cut to the beat rather than ringing its full two and a half seconds, so the chords stay distinct when the tempo is fast. Press Play again to stop. A single chord can be heard on its own from the readout: click its chip and that chord's voices sound together, the chip and its column lit while they ring. Only a chord that is right can be clicked — three notes or four, every one a tone of the chord — so a chip for a chord still being built sits there inert. Pressing Play takes over from a chip, and the chips light in turn as it walks the progression.
- **Checking draws what it found.** When the checklist catches a parallel fifth or octave, a red line strokes itself along each of the two voices that moved in lockstep, from the one chord to the next — so the fault is on the staff, not just described. A run with nothing to fix draws the opposite: all four voices in green, chord to chord the length of the progression, so the shape of the lines just written is there to be looked at — which is the thing four-part writing is really teaching the eye. Either way the lines belong to the report, so editing any note takes them away with it.
- **Up to four voices to a chord.** Each chord takes at most four notes, SATB, and three is enough for it to count as written. Once it has them the ghost preview stops offering a fifth and a note dragged in from elsewhere is turned away. A cross sits under any chord you have written a note into — one note is enough — and clicking it empties that chord, so a voicing can be restarted without clearing the whole staff. It sits below the chord's spelling rather than above the staff, where a stray click would land on a soprano pitch and take the chord with it. Cleared notes drift up and fade rather than blinking out, whether they go one chord at a time or all at once.
- **Place notes directly on the staff.** Hover near the correct system and a faded preview snaps to the nearest line or space; click to drop it in. Drag a placed note to move it — to another pitch or another chord — and double-click it to take it off. The cursor turns to a hand over a note so you can see what is draggable.
- **Real notation.** Engraved treble and bass clefs and the correct key signature for whichever key you picked. The half of the system you are hovering decides the staff: the treble covers C4 up to C6, the bass E2 up to C4, so between them every SATB pitch has a home and none of them piles up ledger lines. Middle C belongs to both — written below the treble staff or above the bass, at its own height in each, the way a widely spaced grand staff actually reads.
- **The checklist.** **Check voice leading** appears once every chord is a chord — three notes or four, all of them tones of that chord, nothing missing that it needs. Until then there is nothing to say about the voice leading, so the button stays away and a line underneath says what is wanted. A later chord written in three parts is allowed — it simply says so, since spacing, crossing and the parallel checks all want a fourth voice. The opening chord is the exception and always needs its four, being the chord that names the voices the rest are read against. Voices come from the opening chord as described above — the report marks what to fix in red, what to watch in amber, and what is merely worth noting in green:
  - **Completeness** — the root and the third must be present somewhere in the four voices; missing either is an error, though the button will not offer itself in that state anyway. The fifth may be left out: with the root tripled that is noted as the usual way to do it, and without the tripling it is a caution rather than an error. A diminished triad is the exception — vii°, and ii° in minor, must keep the fifth, since the diminished fifth is the interval the chord is named for.
  - **Doubling** — reports which tone is doubled. Doubling the third of a major triad is flagged as the weaker choice rather than an error, the root or fifth being preferred; in a minor triad a doubled third is ordinary and passes without comment. Three copies of the third draw the flag whatever the quality, since that is a chord missing something else.
  - **Voice crossing** — a voice sounding above the one named over it (tenor above alto, say) is an error, reported with both notes.
  - **Spacing** — Soprano–Alto and Alto–Tenor each stay within an octave. Tenor–Bass has no limit. A gap that is too wide is named as an interval as well as measured: “a major tenth apart — C4 to E5, 16 semitones”.
  - **Leading-tone doubling** — a doubled leading tone in a V is a caution to watch on the next move.
  - **Leading-tone resolution**, whenever a V moves to the tonic: undoubled, it must rise by step; doubled, at least one copy must rise and the other must either rise too or drop by leap to the fifth of the tonic — the recognised exception.
  - **Parallel fifths and octaves** against the previous chord, across all six voice pairs. Flagged only when the same perfect interval appears in both chords *and* both voices moved the same way. Contrary and oblique motion into a repeated perfect interval are left alone, and so are hidden (direct) fifths and octaves.

## How to use it

1. Pick the key from the circle of fifths — majors outside, relative minors inside. Or take a drill and skip straight to the staff with one voice already written.
2. Tap the Roman numerals to build your progression, then **Begin voicing**.
3. For each chord, place its notes: hover and click to add, drag to move, double-click to remove. Keyboard: Tab to the staff, arrows for chord and pitch, Enter to place or remove.
4. **Play** hears back whatever is on the staff. With all four voices in every chord, **Check voice leading** marks it. Fix what it flags and check again — editing any note clears the last report.
5. At the end, start a new progression to try another key or shape.

## Scope

The checklist reports in prose, with the parallels it finds also drawn on the staff.

Triads only, no sevenths. All seven diatonic degrees are available; in minor, V and vii° raise the leading tone and the rest stay diatonic to the natural minor scale. A progression is built from plain degrees, but the voicing decides the inversion and the label follows it.

## Tech

Single self-contained HTML file. No build step, no external services — just open it in a browser, or visit the [published page](https://shanedoyledev.github.io/voice-leader/), which GitHub Pages serves straight from `index.html` on `main`: a push is a deploy. Everything is drawn as SVG: staff lines, stems, ledger lines and the brace are generated in code, while the clefs, accidentals and noteheads are real engraved outlines. All the theory (scales, chord spelling, the rule checks) is plain JavaScript with no external libraries.

The clef, accidental (sharp, flat, natural, double sharp) and notehead outlines were extracted from [Bravura](https://github.com/steinbergmedia/bravura), Steinberg's reference SMuFL font, and inlined as SVG path data — about 5KB for the seven glyphs — so the page loads no font and makes no network request, but the notation is the genuine engraved shapes. Bravura is © Steinberg Media Technologies GmbH, licensed under the SIL Open Font License 1.1.

The note sounds are five samples from the [Salamander Grand Piano](https://archive.org/details/SalamanderGrandPianoV3) by Alexander Holm (CC-BY 3.0) — C2, C3, C4, C5 and C6, trimmed to their first 2.4 seconds at MP3 frame boundaries and embedded as base64, about 84KB in total. A pitch plays whichever sample is nearest, resampled by no more than six semitones, so it still sounds like a piano rather than a tape effect. Like the notation, this means no network request at runtime.
